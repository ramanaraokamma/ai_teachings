Start with the research problem

A pilot is a rehearsal of the measurement and study procedure. Can the team capture records, interpret the rubric and calculate the planned result? Discovering a defect is useful development evidence. Once that evidence guides a repair, it is no longer an untouched evaluation of the final procedure. Save what failed, what changed and why, then freeze the revised method before using newly reserved primary cases.

Original concept explanation

A pilot checks feasibility, measurement and failure handling before the primary study. Pilot results used for revision are development evidence.

    Separate pictured inputs and complete explanation

    A pilot tests the procedure. A pilot checks whether the team can score answers consistently before running the main study.

    Two reviewers score four practice answers. They agree on three and disagree on one; these answers are excluded from final evaluation.

    The separate picture gives four practice answers scored by two reviewers. Three agreements out of four are 75% observed agreement; one disagreement is 25%. We are not told which answer differs, the label categories, who is right or why they disagreed. Inspect the actual scoring record and rubric before choosing a repair. Agreement does not establish correctness, and four answers do not establish long-run reliability.

    If these answers help revise the rubric or procedure, retain them as development evidence and keep them separate from untouched final evaluation. Adding the corrected practice cases to the final set would blur that role boundary.

    Read the labelled representation

    A pilot can reveal missing instructions, practical delays or ambiguous scoring. It is preparation, not an untouched final estimate after the team uses it to revise methods.

    Original qualification and repair setup

    A pilot may assess feasibility but is not automatically powered or independent evidence for the final hypothesis.

    A useful check asks what would make the reasoning fail. Distinguish a failure to execute the declared procedure from a weakness in the procedure itself.

    The first may require a correction; the second requires an explicitly revised method and new evidence. Preserve unsuccessful cases so an improved result cannot hide the original problem.
