Notice the evidence

In Week 12 every update used both training examples. We now keep the same network h = ReLU(x), y = vh, with only v trained, and divide the data into batches. In this lesson each nonempty batch produces exactly one update. An epoch visits every training example exactly once, without replacement. These rules make the counts unambiguous.

Imagine five fictional records A through E in fixed order. With batch size 2, the groups are [A, B], [C, D] and [E]. Keep the smaller last batch. One epoch has three batches and three updates; two epochs have six updates and ten example visits.

Student lesson · 3 of 14

See how it works

Use the two fictional pairs A = (1, 2) and B = (2, 4), start v = 0 and rate 0.1. For one pair with hidden value h, squared-error gradient is 2 times (vh - target) times h. For a batch, average these gradients at the same current v. Apply one update, then recompute for the next batch.

def epoch(v, rows, size, rate):
    if not rows or size < 1:
        raise ValueError("Need rows and positive batch size")
    trace = []
    for start in range(0, len(rows), size):
        batch = rows[start:start + size]
        hs = [max(0.0, x) for x, target in batch]
        gradient = sum(2 * (v * h - target) * h
                       for h, (x, target) in zip(hs, batch)) / len(batch)
        v = v - rate * gradient
        trace.append(v)
    return trace

rows = [(1.0, 2.0), (2.0, 4.0)]
print([round(v, 6) for v in epoch(0.0, rows, 1, 0.1)])  # [0.4, 1.68]
print([round(v, 6) for v in epoch(0.0, rows, 2, 0.1)])  # [1.0]

Assume finite numeric pairs, integer size and finite rate. With size 1, A gives gradient -4 and new v = 0.4. B then uses 0.4, giving prediction 0.8, residual -3.2 and gradient -12.8, so v becomes 1.68. With size 2, both gradients are evaluated at v = 0; their mean is -10, producing v = 1.

A pictorial worked case: Batching and epochs

Show why an epoch’s example order can matter for single-example updates.

The facts we will use

Toy y=wx, rate 0.1, squared loss, start w = 0. Training A=(x = 1, target 2), B=(x = 2, target 0).

All inputs and outcomes in this worked example are invented classroom data; no real model run is claimed.

Why this result follows

A single-example update changes the weight before the next example’s gradient is calculated. Reversing the example order can therefore change the path. A full-batch update computes both gradients at the same starting weight and averages them before changing that weight. The distinction is when the update occurs, not whether the examples have different labels.

What this example does not establish

Different order effects do not mean a universally better batching strategy; compare declared schedules and budgets fairly.

Student lesson · 7 of 14

Find and repair a mistake

Batch losses observed during training can refer to different parameter values and different examples. They are not automatically the full training loss of the final model. To compare final models, freeze each one and evaluate every training example again. Here full training MSE is 2.5 times (v - 2) squared: at v = 1 it is 2.5, and at v = 1.68 it is 0.256. These are training results only.

Even at one fixed model, averaging batch means without their sizes gives the wrong dataset mean when batches have different lengths. Suppose the squared errors on A to E are [1, 1, 4, 4, 9]. Batch means for lengths 2, 2, 1 are [1, 4, 9]. Full MSE is (2 times 1 + 2 times 4 + 1 times 9) / 5 = 3.8, not (1 + 4 + 9) / 3. Use example counts as weights.

Student lesson · 12 of 14

Show what you know

Equal epochs mean equal example visits under our rule, but changing batch size changes the number of updates. A comparison should report both counts, starting values, order, learning rate and the rule for the last batch. Neither smaller batches nor more updates guarantee better independent performance.
