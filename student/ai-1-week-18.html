Main lesson · Section 2

Read the definition before interpreting the number

A score, a probability claim and confident wording can describe different things.

Separate the claim and its outcome check

A prediction can be expressed strongly and still be wrong. Confidence describes a claim about support or likelihood; correctness is checked against evidence.

Keep the prediction, the confidence claim and the observed outcome in separate columns. None can replace the others.

Inspect the meaning of an 80 display

A display reading 80 might mean 80 out of 100 points for matching features, an estimated 80 percent chance of a specified event, or something else. Read its definition before interpreting it.

The number alone does not tell you which meaning applies. A sentence such as definitely far is not a measured probability.

Keep invented probabilities out of Ramp1

Ramp1 from Week 17 produced categories using a threshold. It supplied no probability estimates. We must not attach a probability merely because the height is far from that threshold.

This week uses fictional forecast records with an explicitly stated probability claim; we are checking those claims, not inventing them for Ramp1.

Explain what each claim requires

  • What event does a probability estimate refer to? How would you distinguish an 80-point matching score from an estimated 80 percent chance of correctness? Does Ramp1 supply either probability estimate?

Main lesson · Section 3

Compare the probability claim with groups of outcomes

A finite group supplies evidence without guaranteeing an exact future count.

Read three different evidence situations

Explain what an 80 percent claim estimates

An 80 percent claim means an estimated chance of 80 out of 100, for the event that was named. Across many relevant cases carrying that claim, a well-calibrated method should be correct about that often.

It does not promise exactly eight successes in every ten cases or tell us which cases will fail.

Compare both observed groups within their limits

Eight correct out of ten agrees numerically with 80 percent in this small group. Five out of ten is lower. Both are observations worth reporting; neither small group alone establishes how dependable the method will be.

Larger relevant checks, independent of model fitting and score adjustment, give a stronger basis for judging the claim.

Main lesson · Section 7

Seek evidence instead of stronger wording

Read the score definition and request relevant outcome checks.

Ask what the display measures and how it was checked

Before relying on a confidence display, ask what its number means, how it was checked on relevant cases and what happens when it is wrong. A feature-match score may help rank options without being a probability.

A generated sentence may sound certain without supplying either a defined score or supporting evidence.

Keep repeated agreement and permission separate from truth

Repeating a claim does not establish its truth. Several tools can share information and mistakes. Useful checks seek relevant independent evidence and preserve unresolved outcomes.

Review decisions also depend on the consequences of an error; a probability alone does not grant permission to act.

Repair the invented 100-percent claim

  • CLAIM A Higher confidence always means more correct.

  • CLAIM B A correct low-confidence answer proves confidence is useless.

STRONG ANSWER FRAME I claim ______ because the evidence shows ______. The process is ______. One limit is ______.
