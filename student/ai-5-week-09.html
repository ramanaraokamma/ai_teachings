Student lesson · 2 of 14

Notice the evidence

Let a one-example model predict w and let its target be 2. Its squared loss is L(w) = (w - 2) squared. Here w is the parameter we vary, not a new data example. L is a nonnegative function of that parameter.

From w = 0 to w = 1, the loss changes by -3 while w changes by 1. The average slope is -3. From w = 1 to w = 2 it is -1. The curve does not have one fixed slope everywhere.

Student lesson · 3 of 14

See how it works

Near w = 1, use two symmetric nearby values: 0.9 and 1.1. Their losses are 1.21 and 0.81. Change in loss divided by change in w gives (0.81 - 1.21) / (1.1 - 0.9) = -2. This symmetric estimate describes change near the centre w = 1.

def loss(w):
    return (w - 2) ** 2

def local_slope(w, step):
    if step <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Step must be positive")
    return (loss(w + step) - loss(w - step)) / (2 * step)

for w in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(w, round(local_slope(w, 0.1), 6))
# Slopes near these centres: -2, 0, 2

For this quadratic, the symmetric estimate equals the exact slope 2(w - 2) in exact arithmetic. Numerical estimates for other functions are generally approximate. Smaller steps can reduce some approximation errors, but extremely small steps can suffer from computer rounding.

A pictorial worked case: Slopes and local change

Use a local slope to propose a small move rather than claiming every move helps.

The facts we will use

Toy loss J(a)=(a-2)². At a = 0, use symmetric step 0.5.

All inputs and outcomes in this worked example are invented classroom data; no real model run is claimed.

Why this result follows

A slope describes how the loss changes near the current parameter. The symmetric calculation compares equal small moves on either side. Its negative result suggests a small increase may help at zero. It does not promise every increase helps: jumping to five passes the optimum at two and produces a larger loss. Check the new loss after each proposed step.

What this example does not establish

A finite difference approximates local change; numerical precision and nonsmooth points need care in more complex objectives.

Student lesson · 7 of 14

Find and repair a mistake

A negative slope at w = 1 suggests that a sufficiently small increase reduces this smooth loss locally. Moving to 1.1 lowers loss from 1 to 0.81. A jump all the way to 4 raises loss to 4. The initial slope cannot justify a step of any size.

A zero slope is also limited evidence. The nonnegative function F(w) = (w squared - 1) squared has zero local slope at w = 0, yet F(0) = 1 and F(0.1) = 0.9801. Small moves on either side lower that function. A flat first-order clue can occur at a point that is not a minimum.

Locate the earliest unsupported step

Starting at w = 3, compare loss after moving to 2.9 and after moving to -1. Both moves decrease w.

Student lesson · 12 of 14

Show what you know

Always record the function, centre, nearby values and denominator used in a slope estimate. An average slope across a wide interval is not automatically the local slope at either endpoint. Do not compare slope magnitudes without considering parameter and loss units.
