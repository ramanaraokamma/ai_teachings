Vary one parameter and record its loss

The horizontal coordinate is a parameter,not a new data example.

Declare the one-example model and squared loss

Let a one-example model predict w and let its target be 2. Its squared loss is L(w) = (w - 2) squared. Here w is the parameter we vary, not a new data example. L is a nonnegative function of that parameter.

Compute average slopes on named intervals

From w = 0 to w = 1, the loss changes by -3 while w changes by 1. The average slope is -3. From w = 1 to w = 2 it is -1. The curve does not have one fixed slope everywhere.

    Divide the loss change by the full horizontal gap

    Keep the centre and both nearby evaluations explicit.

    Complete near-w1calculation

    Near w = 1, use two symmetric nearby values: 0.9 and 1.1. Their losses are 1.21 and 0.81. Change in loss divided by change in w gives (0.81 - 1.21) / (1.1 - 0.9) = -2.

    This symmetric estimate describes change near the centre w = 1.

    Spacing,denominator and precision contract

    The symmetric formula is[L(w+h)−L(w−h)]/(2h), centred at w. Its h is a measurement spacing for estimating slope, not a training learning rate. At w1, h.1, the full horizontal gap is.2, not.1. For this quadratic the symmetric estimate matches 2(w−2)in exact arithmetic; other functions generally have approximation error. Extremely small spacing can also lose numerical precision.

    Record centre, spacing, both losses and the full denominator.

    For this quadratic, the symmetric estimate equals the exact slope 2(w - 2) in exact arithmetic. Numerical estimates for other functions are generally approximate.

    Smaller steps can reduce some approximation errors, but extremely small steps can suffer from computer rounding.

    Runnable positive-spacing estimator

    def loss(w):
    return (w - 2) ** 2

def local_slope(w, step):
    if step <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Step must be positive")
    return (loss(w + step) - loss(w - step)) / (2 * step)

for w in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(w, round(local_slope(w, 0.1), 6))

    Main lesson · Section 7

    Repair “any step helps”and “zero slope means minimum”

    Use complete numerical counterexamples and labelled curves.

    Small-step versus jump and zero-slope counterexamples

    A negative slope at w = 1 suggests that a sufficiently small increase reduces this smooth loss locally. Moving to 1.1 lowers loss from 1 to 0.81. A jump all the way to 4 raises loss to 4.

    The initial slope cannot justify a step of any size.

    A zero slope is also limited evidence. The nonnegative function F(w) = (w squared - 1) squared has zero local slope at w = 0, yet F(0) = 1 and F(0.1) = 0.9801. Small moves on either side lower that function.

    A flat first-order clue can occur at a point that is not a minimum.

    Two labelled loss curves

    Try both decreasing moves fromw3
