Main lesson · Section 2

Understand the question and evidence contract

Preserve units, case counts and uncertainty limits without making a figure stand in for missing evidence.

Start with the research problem

A chart changes how easy a difference is to see; it does not add observations. Label what the axis measures, how many cases contributed and which conditions were evaluated. For ordinary magnitude bars, a zero baseline preserves the meaning of bar length. A focused difference plot can use another clearly explained scale, but hidden truncation makes a small difference look much larger.

Keep missing uncertainty missing rather than decorating a plot with invented error bars.

Original concept explanation

Figures should preserve denominators, scales and uncertainty rather than exaggerate the preferred comparison. A plot is a representation of evidence, not extra evidence.

    Compare the intermediate evidence with the final statement. Explain why each number or decision follows from the stated inputs.

    The conclusion belongs to this scenario and procedure; changing the inputs, evaluation population or authority can change what it establishes. Retain the raw record so a later revision can be compared honestly.

    Separate pictured inputs and complete explanation

    Draw bars that preserve the comparison. A report plots correct counts for two versions on the same ten cases.

    A has 6/10 correct; B has 8/10. The count axis should start at zero and identify the ten-case denominator.

    The separate picture compares six versus eight correct on the same ten cases. A is 60%, B 80%; the difference is two correct or twenty percentage points. Use count bars from zero to ten and label the common ten-case denominator. If the bars secretly start at five, their visible heights are one and three, creating a threefold height ratio even though the actual count ratio is eight divided by six.

    The new native figure keeps main supplied percentages separate from the ten-case count example. No uncertainty bounds are supplied, and a well-labelled chart does not turn ten cases into a population or causal conclusion.

    Read the labelled representation

    Figures make evidence easier to inspect when labels and scales preserve meaning. A dramatic-looking visual cannot supply missing uncertainty or representativeness.

    Find the first unsupported inference and repair it

    Use the supplied conditions rather than inventing missing evidence.

    Original qualification and repair setup

    Visual choices must reflect the intended metric and cannot imply causal certainty from association.

    A useful check asks what would make the reasoning fail. Distinguish a failure to execute the declared procedure from a weakness in the procedure itself.

    The first may require a correction; the second requires an explicitly revised method and new evidence. Preserve unsuccessful cases so an improved result cannot hide the original problem.
