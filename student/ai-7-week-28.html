Understand the question and evidence contract

Start with the research problem

A result is informative even when it does not meet the original expectation. Start with the criterion that was recorded before results, then state what the supplied evidence actually shows. An observed tie fails a strict-improvement prediction, but it does not prove equal population performance.

A useful next question can follow; label it new work rather than replacing the original hypothesis with one chosen to fit the outcome.

Original concept explanation

Negative results report that the evidence did not meet a declared expectation. They can reveal design limits or useful constraints without proving no effect anywhere.

    Separate pictured inputs and complete explanation

    A negative result changes the claim. A proposed prompt improvement ties the baseline on a fixed eight-case evaluation.

    A and B each score 6/8. Before running, the team predicted B would score higher.

    The separate eight-case picture gives A and B six correct each, or 75%. The observed difference is zero, so the prediction that B scores higher is not supported on this fixed set. The aggregate tie does not tell us whether the same cases were correct or whether failure patterns differ. It is not an equivalence test: uncertainty and a practical equivalence margin are unspecified.

    A new task chosen because A fails can demonstrate that particular selected behavior if B wins, but outcome-driven selection prevents treating it as fresh representative confirmation. Keep the eight-case tie and selected demonstration as separate records.

    Read the labelled representation

    A negative result can be informative when methods and limits are clear. A tie does not prove the methods are equivalent under all conditions or that the experiment was worthless.

    Main lesson · Section 7

    Find the first unsupported inference and repair it

    Use the supplied conditions rather than inventing missing evidence.

    Original qualification and repair setup

    Failure to meet a criterion or reject a null is not proof of exact equivalence.

    A useful check asks what would make the reasoning fail. Distinguish a failure to execute the declared procedure from a weakness in the procedure itself.

    The first may require a correction; the second requires an explicitly revised method and new evidence. Preserve unsuccessful cases so an improved result cannot hide the original problem.
