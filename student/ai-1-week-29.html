Begin the final paper activity-recommender sequence

Use the supplied fictional log; a recommendation remains a choice.

Meet the users and project sequence

For the final project sequence, we will develop a small activity recommender on paper. Its users are fictional pupils choosing a classroom activity that fits their available time and room conditions.

We will decide what the recommender must do, build a traceable process, test it and explain its limits. A recommendation is a suggestion; the pupil can decline it.

Read the ten-request observation

Start before the solution. Our supplied observation log describes ten fictional requests made using an activity catalogue. Six first choices fit the request’s time and room conditions. Four did not and needed another choice.

These invented classroom records give us a problem to investigate. They are not observations of your classmates and do not show how often this happens in other classes.

Write only what the evidence establishes

A useful problem statement is: “In the supplied ten-request exercise, four first choices did not fit the stated time or room conditions, so those users had to choose again.” “We need AI” names a possible technology but says neither who needs help nor what should improve.

The log also does not establish whether unclear wording, missing information or another cause produced the mismatches.

Main lesson · Section 3

Define useful outcomes before building

Check compatibility and honest no-match results without promising enjoyment.

Separate observation, need and project boundary

Define compatibility and no-match success

Our target outcome is a compatible suggestion when the catalogue contains one, and a clear no-match message when none fits.

Compatibility means respecting the stated conditions; it does not mean that every pupil will enjoy the activity. We can check time and room conditions from a request and catalogue. Enjoyment would need different evidence.

Count helpful results as well as wrong suggestions

  • Count compatible suggestions, incompatible suggestions and no-match messages separately. Otherwise a system could avoid every wrong suggestion by refusing to suggest anything.

    If a valid option existed, that refusal would still fail to provide the intended help. Record the total number of requests so a count has a clear denominator.

  • Point to the input, the deciding step and the result in this week’s visual. Explain the same step in ordinary words. A picture helps when you can say what each part represents.

Specify what would count against your solution

Keep motivation records separate from evaluation of a tool built later.

Compare against a useful simple method

Before building, say what would count against your idea. If a clear catalogue already produces compatible choices for all supplied cases, adding a recommender might offer little benefit on those cases.

If the recommender hides valid options or produces unexplained no-match messages, revise it or keep the simpler method. A project can succeed as an investigation even when the evidence favours a simpler solution.

Label observation, plan and evaluation separately

Keep the ten-request observation log separate from later evaluation records. It motivates the problem; it is not a fair test result for a tool that has not been built.

Later weeks will introduce explicit requirements and test cases, including a reserved final set. Label plans as plans and measured results as results.

Main lesson · Section 8

Practise: separate findings from hypotheses

A mismatch count does not establish everyone’s feelings or its cause.

Repair the unsupported catalogue story

Suppose a team writes “All pupils hate the catalogue because it is too long.” The supplied log says nothing about every pupil’s feelings or the cause of the mismatches.

A more careful note is “Four of ten first choices were incompatible; the cause is not yet known.” A hypothesis such as “the time information may be hard to find” is something to investigate, not an established finding.

Propose a permitted investigation without collecting new personal data

If we later investigate the cause, we could inspect where compatibility information appears in the catalogue or have a teacher organise a permitted comparison of clearer formats.

Do not quietly collect real pupils’ data just because it might help. For this sequence, the supplied fictional records are enough to build and test a paper process.

Investigate a need through a supplied role-play

The three fictional evidence cards

Fictional need-finding role-play: use these supplied statements rather than classmates’ personal experiences. Requester card: “I had ten minutes and needed a quiet activity. My first choice took twenty minutes, so I chose again.” Catalogue card: “Durations and room conditions are listed, but this record does not show why the requester missed them.” Observer card: “Across ten fictional requests, six first choices fit and four did not.”

Ask, distinguish evidence and frame the goal

  1. One partner reads the requester card and the other asks neutral questions: What were you trying to do? What happened next? What information would you need to check a compatible choice? Answer only from the cards and mark absent answers unknown. Each learner writes a need statement, a possible explanation labelled hypothesis, a measurable outcome and a simple checklist alternative. Swap roles. Do not claim the role-play establishes real pupils’ preferences or the effectiveness of an unbuilt recommender.

  2. Keep a normal case and an edge case clearly separated.

  3. Record what happened—even when it does not match your prediction.

  4. Explain the result, one limitation, and the next useful test.
