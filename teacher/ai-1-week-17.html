Explain the mechanism

Model Prediction Is Not Fact using the supplied case, labelled mechanism and reasoning. Make the scope of each conclusion explicit.

Follow the information instead of imagining magic inside the machine.

Key idea

CORE IDEA A prediction is a fallible output supported by clues.

How the mechanism works

UNDER THE HOOD Prediction estimates a target that is unknown at decision time. Evidence supports the estimate; ground truth is the later or independently established outcome.

Treating predictions as labels can create self-confirming records.

Model answer

MODEL REASONING Mark model or rule outputs as predictions, observable clues as evidence, and independently checked outcomes as facts.

A prediction becomes evaluated against a fact; it does not become a fact merely because time passes.

A claim to avoid

DO NOT OVERCLAIM Do not copy a model prediction into the answer column and later call it ground truth.

Explain the added worked example

A prediction states what a model outputs. An observation records what was measured or happened. To check a prediction, keep both records and compare the same case under the same outcome rule.

Writing a prediction into the observation column would make the check circular.

Explain the added worked example · 1

Use a fictional ramp investigation. Before each trial, Ramp1 predicts far for a ramp height of at least 6 cm and near otherwise. Its allowed heights are whole centimetres from 1 through 10.

After a toy rolls, a learner measures its travel distance. The outcome rule is far for a distance of at least 4 floor units and near otherwise. Ramp height and travel distance are different quantities.

Explain the added worked example · 2

The values in this lesson are invented practice records, not a physical law about ramps. Ramp1 is a fixed paper model for learning how to check predictions.

Each trial has an ID so that its height, prediction and later measurement stay together. Keep the model version and record the prediction before opening the observation card.

Explain the added worked example · 3

For B, the input 6 cm produces the prediction far. The later distance of 3 units produces the observed category near. This is a mismatch. Retain far as the original prediction and near as the observed category.

Changing the old prediction to near would hide the mistake rather than improve the original result.

Explain the added worked example · 4

For A and C, the categories match. These two matches provide evidence about these trials. They do not show that every future prediction will be correct.

Even a correctly computed prediction can disagree with an observation because following the model rule and matching the world are different checks.

Classroom dialogue

Ask the class

Ask: Which facts are given, and which would we have to assume? Learners mark relevant details before discussing answers.

Language to model

Say: Predict first. Show the rule, information path or calculation that would produce the result. A correct guess is not yet an explanation.

Reveal reasoning one step at a time. Explain why a tempting alternative fails. Write units and denominators for arithmetic, trace conditions for decisions, and identify supporting sources for claims.

In pairs, one learner solves while the other checks evidence; exchange roles. Remove the worked answer before independent assessment.

Model these guided questions

A predicts near, observed near, match; B far, observed near, mismatch; C far, observed far, match. D predicts near with missing distance; E predicts far with conflicting 3 and 4; both unassessed.

Two matches out of three assessed, two unassessed. Exit: changing B after reading the outcome hides a mismatch; retain its original far prediction and observed near outcome, and identify any revised model separately.

Find the unsupported claim

CLAIM TO REPAIR A prediction is a fact because a computer produced it.

Address these additional misconceptions

Running the rule correctly proves the prediction correct: Rule execution and agreement with the observed outcome are separate checks.

A missing outcome is a success or a failure: Mark it unassessed and report the missing count alongside assessed results.

Every recorded observation must be true: Check the trial identity, units and evidence; document supported corrections.

A new trial repairs the old observation: A new trial has its own ID and result; the old outcome may remain unknown.

Explanations for the additional workbook examples

W1 Separate inputs from observed outcomes

Workbook question · W1

For Ramp1 trial B, height is 6 cm and the later travel distance is 3 units. State the input, prediction, observation, observed category and comparison status.

Explain why the prediction rule and outcome rule use different measurements.

Teacher reasoning · W1

Input height 6 cm; prediction far because 6 is at least 6; observed distance 3 units; observed category near because 3 is below 4; mismatch.

Height is available to the model before the trial, while travel distance is measured afterward to check its prediction.

W3 Keep incomplete evidence visible

Workbook question · W3

D predicts near but has no distance record. E predicts far but its two distance records say 3 and 4 units.

Teacher reasoning · W3

W5 Correct a report without erasing evidence

Workbook question · W5

After seeing B’s observed near category, a learner replaces Ramp1’s far prediction with near. They call the new entry a corrected observation and report a perfect result for A through C.

05. Connect the explanation to its limit

The moisture score generates a prediction; it is not the observed drying time. The later measurement supplies separate evidence under the stated observation policy. Save both values and their units.

Altering the recorded time to match D would destroy the evaluation rather than repair the model. Limit: One failure does not prove every prediction is wrong; one agreement does not establish reliability on future trials.
