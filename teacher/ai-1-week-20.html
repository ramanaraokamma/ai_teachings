Main lesson · Section 2

Teach requirements, ranking and feedback as separate checks

Use the exact rule to explain both exclusions and remaining preference tradeoffs.

Begin with BookRec1’s complete task

A recommendation suggests an option for a particular purpose. Start with requirements: conditions every suggested item must meet. Then use preferences to rank the eligible options.

An item that fails a requirement cannot make up for it by collecting more preference points.

A fictional reader needs an available English book. The reader prefers topic space and format comic. BookRec1 gives eligible books 2 points for space and 1 for comic; a known mismatch gives 0.

It returns every eligible book tied for the highest score. If none are eligible, it reports no eligible option rather than ignoring a requirement.

The candidates are J: space, prose, English, available; K: animals, comic, English, available; L: space, comic, Spanish, available; M: space, comic, English, unavailable. These are invented records.

The purpose is to trace a recommendation, not to infer anything about real students or collect their personal preferences.

Trace the eligible scores and faithful explanation

J passes both requirements and scores 2. K passes and scores 1. L fails the English requirement; M fails availability. Do not place L or M above J because they would earn 3 preference points.

They are excluded before the eligible scores are compared.

A faithful explanation says J is available in English and has the highest eligible score because its space topic matches the stated preference. It should also mention that J is prose, not comic.

Claiming that the system knows the reader will love J would go beyond the computation and the evidence.

Model the separate break chooser

A fictional classroom break chooser recommends a suitable paper activity.

Must be quiet and take at most 12 minutes. Score 2 for puzzles and 1 for solo.

A puzzle,solo,noisy,8 min; B puzzle,group,quiet,12 min; C drawing,solo,quiet,10 min.

A hard requirement determines whether an option can be chosen at all. Preferences rank the eligible options. The apparent best preference match, A, is not suitable under the quiet requirement.

If a learner clicks C instead, the event alone does not tell us whether they disliked B, misclicked or wanted variety.

Change only the maximum time

  1. Change: Change the maximum time to 10 minutes.

  2. Prediction: Only C remains eligible and is recommended.

  3. Reason: B takes 12 minutes; A is still noisy.

Limit the satisfaction claim from a click

One click is an observed event, not proof of satisfaction or a stable personal preference.

Audit what exposure and recorded events establish

Recorded eventWhat it establishesWhat it does not establish

Reader said J was too longA stated reason in this contextAll space books are unwanted

A click can reflect interest, curiosity or a mistaken tap. No click can reflect a missed display, lack of time or a decision not to choose it. Preserve the observed event separately from any interpretation.

More direct feedback may clarify a reason, but one response still describes one person and occasion.

Useful feedback records name the item, what was shown, the system version, the event and any stated reason. Use only information needed for the task.

Our paper exercises use invented feedback; they do not require accounts or recording classmates’ browsing behaviour.

Explain the mechanism

Follow the information instead of imagining magic inside the machine.

1. Input — information entering

INPUT reader goals and candidate-book traits

2. Process — the deciding step

PROCESS score and rank candidates with chosen tradeoffs

3. Output — the result

OUTPUT ordered recommendations plus reasons

CORE IDEA A recommender ranks possibilities using goals, information, and tradeoffs.

UNDER THE HOOD Recommendation is ranking, not merely classification. An objective combines relevance signals and constraints; tradeoffs may include familiarity, diversity, novelty, safety, and availability.

The top score reflects the objective, not universal quality.

Draw a fresh example with all three stages:

Every step connects a claim to observable evidence.

CASE Two readers can receive different book rankings because their goals, reading level, interests, and novelty preferences differ.

STEP 1 — OBSERVE reader goals and candidate-book traits

STEP 2 — TRACE score and rank candidates with chosen tradeoffs

STEP 3 — CONCLUDE ordered recommendations plus reasons

MODEL REASONING Use the same transparent scoring method with each reader's stated preferences. Different inputs create different totals. Report tradeoffs, ties, and one option outside the top rank to preserve choice.

DO NOT OVERCLAIM A recommendation optimizes chosen goals and data; it does not discover what a person truly wants.
