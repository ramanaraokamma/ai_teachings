Week 19: Sampling and Representation

TermMeaning
sampleA selected group of observations, or an outcome drawn from a distribution; the context identifies which meaning applies.
populationThe full group of cases or people a study aims to describe.
representationA way to encode information so a person or system can work with it.
sampling biasA systematic mismatch between selected examples and the population the study intends to describe.

A pictorial worked case: Sampling and Representation

A fictional Grade 7 class wants to understand book-format preferences across its whole grade.

The facts we will use

Population: all 60 Grade 7 learners. Frame: current school-approved list of those 60, used only by the teacher.

Teacher randomly invites 15; 12 answer the same neutral comic/prose/either question. No names enter the analysis table.

Why this result follows

A sample plan states who could be selected, how selection happens and what happens after invitation. Random invitations do not guarantee that respondents resemble those who declined. Use a neutral question and preserve nonresponse coverage. Learners can discuss aggregate counts without receiving the identifiable selection frame.

What this example does not establish

Even the random-invitation plan does not remove nonresponse bias or justify revealing identifiable school lists.

Change one thing in the pictured case

Change: Invite only the 15 library-club members instead.

Prediction: The invitation process no longer covers the whole grade through this frame.

Reason: Club members may differ in reading interests; selection is now limited to one group.

Ask while showing the picture

Cover the result. Ask learners to name the inputs and predict the next step. Uncover one step, connect the icon to the actual procedure, and explain its evidence. Then change the supplied condition and compare. The picture is a representation; it does not document execution of a real AI model.

Address these additional misconceptions

A successful run proves the conclusion: Selection and response are separate. Even a fair invitation sample can be distorted if only interested students respond. Report numbers invited and responding and explain who may be absent.

Explanations for the additional workbook examples

W1 Define the population

The question is whether all Grade 7 students prefer a longer lunch. Name the population and one unsuitable sample.

Population: all Grade 7 students. Only lunch-club members or one friend group is an unsuitable basis for a whole-grade claim.

W2 Read the sample result

Seven of 12 fictional respondents prefer longer lunch. State the result without generalizing.

7/12 of these respondents prefer longer lunch, about 58.3 percent.

The numerator is seven respondents choosing longer lunch and the denominator is all twelve observed respondents. The fraction is 7/12, approximately 58.3 percent. Say “of these respondents”; the calculation alone supplies no evidence about the preferences of students who were not represented.

W3 Repair a claim

A report says all Grade 7 prefers coding after surveying only the coding club. Rewrite it.

Among surveyed coding-club respondents, the observed choices favored coding; the result does not establish the whole grade preference.

W4 Plan independently

Design an anonymous sample plan to ask all Grade 7 about three activity choices. State population, sampling frame, selection method, question, and how you will report nonresponse.

Use the full grade roster, teacher-managed random selection or a clearly described balanced plan, neutral identical options, anonymous response records and invited/responded counts. Explain limits of nonresponse and small samples.

W5 Separate invitation and response

Twenty students are selected fairly but only 8 answer. Does fair selection remove the need to report nonresponse?

No. The 12 nonrespondents may differ. Report 8 of 20 and the limitation.

Graphs and honest scales — W1 Read the scale

A bar is 6 squares long with 2 votes per square. How many votes is that?

12 votes.

One square represents two votes. Six equal squares therefore represent 6 times 2 = 12 votes. Ask the learner to name both the visible length and the unit scale before giving the count.

Graphs and honest scales — W2 Compare fairly

Counts are 12 and 15. State the difference and ratio using the true values.

Difference 3; ratio 15/12 = 1.25.

The count difference is 15 - 12 = 3 votes. Taking the larger count relative to the smaller gives 15/12 = 1.25, so 15 is 1.25 times 12, or 25 percent larger. Label which count is the reference for the ratio.

Graphs and honest scales — W3 Repair a misleading graph

Bars for 12 and 15 start at 10. Explain the misleading lengths and repair.

Visible lengths 2 and 5 suggest a 2.5 ratio. Start at zero, use a common scale and label counts 12 and 15.

Graphs and honest scales — W4 Draw independently

Draw a bar chart for Music 6, Art 9, Coding 3. Include title, category labels, vote units, zero baseline and equal scale. State two supported comparisons.

Music 6, Art 9, Coding 3 on one scale. Art has 3 more than Music and three times Coding. Accept other correct comparisons.

Graphs and honest scales — W5 Limit the claim

Art has the most votes among 18 responses. Does that establish Art is best for learning?

No. Preference counts do not measure learning effectiveness or establish a cause.
