Explain the mechanism

Explain the mechanism

Follow the information instead of imagining magic inside the machine.

INPUT specific claim extracted from an output

PROCESS check independent suitable sources and reconcile differences

OUTPUT supported, corrected, uncertain, or removed claim

CORE IDEA Fluent AI output is not evidence; important claims need independent support.

UNDER THE HOOD Verification is claim-level evidence work. Source independence, authority, direct support, recency, and agreement matter. Multiple pages repeating the same upstream error are not independent confirmation.

Draw a fresh example with all three stages:

Every step connects a claim to observable evidence.

CASE A generated paragraph confidently claims that an animal lives in a region; the wording is not evidence that the claim is true.

STEP 1 — OBSERVE specific claim extracted from an output

STEP 2 — TRACE check independent suitable sources and reconcile differences

STEP 3 — CONCLUDE supported, corrected, uncertain, or removed claim

MODEL REASONING Check the exact claim, not the whole paragraph. Use sources with direct relevant evidence, note publication context, and explain whether they independently agree. If evidence is weak or conflicting, revise or remove the claim.

DO NOT OVERCLAIM Two search results are not two independent sources when both copy the same page.

What new evidence could change this answer?

Concrete teaching case

An answer says six groups of four make 28.

Verify independently.

Exact explanation to model

Six times four is 24. Draw six groups or add four six times. Confident wording does not change arithmetic.

Explain the added worked example

An answer can contain a mixture of correct, incorrect and unverified statements. Checking one sentence does not validate every other sentence. First separate the answer into individual claims. Then ask what evidence would support each one: a record, a measurement, a calculation or some combination.

Our fictional ribbon activity uses current materials card R. R states: four rolls of ribbon, each exactly 2 metres long; twelve pieces will each use 50 centimetres; no cutting loss is included in this paper model. One metre equals 100 centimetres. An old card Q describes a different activity with three rolls. These are provided classroom records, not instructions to cut real materials.

A draft says “R gives four rolls. The starting length is 8 metres. The pieces use 600 metres. Two metres remain. Every participant will enjoy the activity.” Do not approve or reject the whole paragraph at once. Inspect the roll count, starting length, used length, remaining length and enjoyment claim separately.

A citation is useful only if you can locate the source and confirm that it supports the specific claim. The title “Materials card R” beside a paragraph does not make the enjoyment claim part of R. If a cited card Z is not supplied or cannot be found, that citation cannot currently verify its claim. Record the gap rather than inventing Z’s contents.

Check whether the source covers the right activity, version, population or time. Q’s three rolls may be accurate for its own activity but irrelevant to the current four-roll task. In real work, also inspect who created the record, how they obtained the information and whether there is a correction or better source.

Model the reasoning aloud

Use the complete worked example below before asking learners to complete W2. Reveal a small part, invite a prediction, then explain the deciding step. These teacher notes contain solutions; do not reveal the independent case answer during modelling.

Starting length4 × 200 cm = 800 cm8 m
Length used12 × 50 cm = 600 cm6 m
Length remaining800 cm − 600 cm = 200 cm2 m

Convert to one length unit before subtracting. Two metres is 200 centimetres, so four rolls give 800 centimetres. The twelve pieces use 600 centimetres, leaving 200 centimetres, or 2 metres. Subtracting the bare numbers 8 − 600 would mix metres with centimetres and would not describe the remaining length correctly.

Use a second route as a check: 50 centimetres is half a metre, so twelve pieces use six metres. Eight minus six leaves two metres. A reasonableness check also asks whether the used length is at most the starting length and whether starting length equals used plus remaining. These checks can reveal mistakes; they do not replace checking the original inputs and assumptions.

A pictorial worked case: Verify Important Outputs

A fictional craft sheet calculates ribbon left after making bookmarks.

The facts we will use

Four rolls each contain exactly 150 cm. Eight bookmarks use exactly 50 cm each.

No other ribbon loss is assumed; no competition result is recorded.

Why this result follows

All arithmetic first uses centimetres. Converting only after subtraction avoids mixing units. The record supports available, used and remaining lengths under its no-loss assumption. It does not support a promise about winning. A numerically correct total elsewhere in a draft cannot rescue a wrong unit or an invented claim.

What this example does not establish

Real cutting losses or inaccurate roll lengths would change this toy calculation; the assumption must be declared.

Change one thing in the pictured case

Change: Make ten bookmarks instead of eight.

Prediction: 100 cm, or 1 m, remains.

Reason: 600−10×50=100 cm.

Ask while showing the picture

Cover the result. Ask learners to name the inputs and predict the next step. Uncover one step, connect the icon to the actual procedure, and explain its evidence. Then change the supplied condition and compare. The picture is a representation; it does not document execution of a real AI model.

Demonstrate and guide practice

Demonstrate and guide practice

Board sequence

Write the given facts: An answer says six groups of four make 28.

Write the question to explain: Verify independently.

Reveal after predictions: Six times four is 24. Draw six groups or add four six times. Confident wording does not change arithmetic.

Keep for an independent attempt: An assistant says the school library opens at 8, but today's official notice says 9. Which source should guide today's visit? Explain the check.

Classroom dialogue

Ask: Which facts are given, and which would we have to assume? Learners mark relevant details before discussing answers.

Say: Predict first. Show the rule, information path or calculation that would produce the result. A correct guess is not yet an explanation.

Reveal reasoning one step at a time. Explain why a tempting alternative fails. Write units and denominators for arithmetic, trace conditions for decisions, and identify supporting sources for claims.

In pairs, one learner solves while the other checks evidence; exchange roles. Remove the worked answer before independent assessment.

Model these guided questions

Four rolls: supported by R. Starting 8 m: supported by 4 × 2 m. Used 600 m: contradicted; it is 600 cm = 6 m. Remaining 2 m: supported by 8 − 6, with no cutting loss. Everyone enjoys it: unresolved. Correctness of one number does not validate another; show each calculation. Exit: loss is 12 × 2 cm = 24 cm. Remaining is 800 − 600 − 24 = 176 cm. The no-cutting-loss assumption changed.

Diagnose and reteach

CLAIM TO REPAIR A confident answer is stronger evidence than a relevant notice.

For guessing, return to given facts and request a trace. For vocabulary without application, use one example and one non-example. For arithmetic errors, check the setup before the calculation. Finish with a different uncoached case before recording mastery.

Address these additional misconceptions

A citation proves every nearby claim: Inspect the actual source and the specific claim it supports.

The number is enough without a unit: 600 cm and 600 m differ by a factor of 100.

An unsupported claim must be false: Unresolved means the supplied evidence does not settle it.

Three copied claims are three independent checks: Find the origin and seek an independent record or calculation.

Explanations for the additional workbook examples

W1 Separate source facts from derived quantities

Using R, identify a directly stated fact and a quantity that must be calculated. Give the source or expression for each. Explain why a citation to R does not verify the claim that every participant will enjoy the activity.

A directly stated fact is four rolls, 2 m per roll or twelve 50 cm pieces. A derived quantity is 8 m starting length, 6 m used or 2 m remaining, supported by the relevant calculation from R. R contains no enjoyment evidence, so citing it does not support everyone enjoying the activity.

W2 Repair a unit error

A report calculates 12 × 50 = 600 and labels the result metres. Use R to correct the unit, convert the result to metres and calculate the remaining length. Give a second arithmetic route or a check using starting, used and remaining lengths.

Each piece is 50 cm, so 12 × 50 cm = 600 cm = 6 m. Starting length is 4 × 2 m = 8 m; remaining is 8 − 6 = 2 m. A check is 6 m used + 2 m remaining = 8 m starting; alternatively twelve half-metre pieces use 6 m.

W3 Do not count copied claims as separate evidence

Three posters say 600 metres were used, but all copied one draft. A fourth poster cites card Z, which is unavailable. Explain why these posters do not settle the quantity. Name the available evidence and check you would use instead.

The first three are copies of one claim, not independent evidence. Z cannot be inspected, so the fourth citation cannot currently verify the result. Inspect supplied R and recompute twelve 50 cm pieces as 600 cm or 6 m; verify source, units and the no-loss assumption.

W4 Audit a new model with mass units

Fictional craft card M states five sealed bags of modelling material at exactly 250 g per bag. Three models each use 300 g, with no other loss. A draft says “M supplies 1250 g; the models use 900 g; 350 kg remains; every model will win a prize.” Check all four claims, show the remaining mass in grams, and explain the status of the prize claim. Use 1 kg = 1000 g.

1250 g is supported by 5 × 250 g; 900 g is supported by 3 × 300 g. Remaining mass is 1250 − 900 = 350 g, so 350 kg is contradicted by the inputs and unit conversion: 350 kg would be 350000 g, greater than the starting mass. The prize claim is unresolved because M gives no prize evidence; it is not established true or false by the material counts.

W5 State what a missing source prevents you from knowing

Another draft claims that the amount left in W4 accounts for all real-world waste, citing a missing instruction card. Explain which result can still be checked from M and which claim remains unresolved. What information would be needed before revising the no-loss model?

The no-other-loss model in M can be checked: 350 g remains under its stated assumptions. The assertion that this includes all real-world waste is unresolved without relevant waste information. Obtain the actual instruction or measurement record specifying additional loss, its units and what stage it covers; do not invent a waste amount or subtract it twice.

Record each dimension as not yet, with support, or independent. Accept a valid alternative procedure with a defensible trace. Do not penalise an honestly recorded model failure if the learner correctly explains its evidence and limits.

Feedback that the learner uses

Name the earliest unsupported step and give one action to repair it. Have the learner make that repair now and explain why. Then assess the relevant skill on a fresh, fully specified case. Confirm it was not previously practised with feedback; solve and check the new case before assigning it. A follow-up design prompt is a starting brief, not automatically an equivalent test.
