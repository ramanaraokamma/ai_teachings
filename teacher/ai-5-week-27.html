Main definitions and complete rankings

Last week, a neighbourhood depended on a chosen distance rule. This week, compare the same fictional vectors in two ways. Squared distance rewards nearby coordinates.

Cosine similarity compares direction and ignores positive changes in length. Neither rule is automatically right for every task.

For Q, length = square root of (3 squared + 4 squared) = square root of 25 = 5. Divide both components by 5 to get its unit vector [0.6, 0.8]. Its length is one because 0.6 squared + 0.8 squared = 1.

A becomes the same unit vector. C becomes [-0.6, -0.8], pointing in the opposite direction.

These supplied coordinates are for arithmetic practice. They are not learned language embeddings. Normalising each row keeps its direction but removes its original length unless that length is saved separately.

Multiply matching components and add to obtain the dot product. Cosine similarity equals dot(Q, candidate) divided by (length(Q) times length(candidate)). Higher cosine means a closer direction.

For A, the dot product is 3 times 6 + 4 times 8 = 50, so cosine = 50 / (5 times 10) = 1. For B it is 9 / 15 = 0.6; for C it is -25 / 25 = -1.

Trace before running: A gives 1, B gives 0.6 and C gives -1. This classroom code assumes finite, moderate numeric inputs. It rejects empty, unequal-length and zero vectors.

Keep full precision for decisions; rounding here is only for display. Cosine lies between -1 and 1 in exact arithmetic and is not a probability.

Unit-distance identity and guided candidate D

After normalising both Q and every candidate, squared distances to A, B and C are 0, 0.8 and 4. For unit vectors, squared distance = 2 - 2 times cosine.

Thus the smallest normalised distance and largest cosine produce the same ranking, including ties, in exact arithmetic.

Guided: D has squared distance 9 + 1 = 10 and cosine 20 / 25 = 0.8. Raw distance prefers D over A (10 versus 25); cosine prefers A (1 versus 0.8).

Exit: Q and A share direction but have different lengths and may represent different totals. Cosine 1 proves aligned nonzero vectors, not equivalent meaning.

Trace the undefined zero-vector replacement

Raw Euclidean distance includes differences in both direction and magnitude. Cosine compares direction using the vector lengths, so a long vector can align perfectly while being far away in raw coordinates.

Declare which question your similarity measure answers. A zero vector has no direction and cannot be divided by its norm for cosine or unit normalisation.

Diagnose the current misconceptions

Cosine is a probability: Its possible negative values and geometric definition show that it is not a calibrated probability.

Normalisation keeps all information: Positive multiples collapse to the same unit vector; preserve length separately if the task needs it.

Any scaling leaves cosine unchanged: Positive scaling preserves direction. Multiplying only one vector by a negative number reverses the cosine sign; zero scaling makes it undefined.
