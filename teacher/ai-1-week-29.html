Explain the mechanism

Follow the information instead of imagining magic inside the machine.

PROCESS identify user, situation, need, and measurable effect

CORE IDEA A strong project begins with a specific user and observable need.

UNDER THE HOOD Problem framing separates evidence about a user's need from assumptions about a solution. A strong statement identifies who, when, what observable difficulty occurs, and how improvement could be measured.

Every step connects a claim to observable evidence.

STEP 2 — TRACE identify user, situation, need, and measurable effect

STEP 3 — CONCLUDE testable problem statement without a preselected solution

MODEL REASONING A strong statement names the user, context, observable difficulty, and desired improvement without requiring AI. Protect privacy, avoid leading questions, and distinguish what the user said from your inference.

Explain the added worked example

For the final project sequence, we will develop a small activity recommender on paper. Its users are fictional pupils choosing a classroom activity that fits their available time and room conditions. We will decide what the recommender must do, build a traceable process, test it and explain its limits. A recommendation is a suggestion; the pupil can decline it.

Start before the solution. Our supplied observation log describes ten fictional requests made using an activity catalogue. Six first choices fit the request’s time and room conditions. Four did not and needed another choice. These invented classroom records give us a problem to investigate. They are not observations of your classmates and do not show how often this happens in other classes.

A useful problem statement is: “In the supplied ten-request exercise, four first choices did not fit the stated time or room conditions, so those users had to choose again.” “We need AI” names a possible technology but says neither who needs help nor what should improve. The log also does not establish whether unclear wording, missing information or another cause produced the mismatches.

Our target outcome is a compatible suggestion when the catalogue contains one, and a clear no-match message when none fits. Compatibility means respecting the stated conditions; it does not mean that every pupil will enjoy the activity. We can check time and room conditions from a request and catalogue. Enjoyment would need different evidence.

Count compatible suggestions, incompatible suggestions and no-match messages separately. Otherwise a system could avoid every wrong suggestion by refusing to suggest anything. If a valid option existed, that refusal would still fail to provide the intended help. Record the total number of requests so a count has a clear denominator.

A pictorial worked case: Find a Real Problem

A fictional class loses time finding the correct craft tray.

The facts we will use

In eight recorded requests, three selected trays lack a required tool; five match.

The teacher permits a paper finder using fictional requests and an approved tray list, with no pupil names.

Why this result follows

The mismatch record describes a concrete need. A measurable goal checks whether the chosen tray satisfies the request; calling the system smart would not tell us how to evaluate it. Start with a transparent paper baseline, and keep unsatisfied requests visible instead of selecting a near match without warning.

What this example does not establish

Eight requests do not show the problem’s frequency across the entire school or prove AI is necessary.

Classroom dialogue

Ask: Which facts are given, and which would we have to assume? Learners mark relevant details before discussing answers.

Say: Predict first. Show the rule, information path or calculation that would produce the result. A correct guess is not yet an explanation.

Reveal reasoning one step at a time. Explain why a tempting alternative fails. Write units and denominators for arithmetic, trace conditions for decisions, and identify supporting sources for claims.

In pairs, one learner solves while the other checks evidence; exchange roles. Remove the worked answer before independent assessment.

Model these guided questions

The brief should identify fictional pupils choosing classroom activities, four incompatible first choices out of ten, and the goal of a compatible suggestion where one exists or an explained no-match result otherwise. Inputs are minutes and quiet-room requirement. Boundaries include fictional requests, teacher-approved catalogue and suggestions only. A clear catalogue or checklist is a simpler alternative. The log does not establish causes, feelings or a future tool’s benefit. Exit: no; always asking avoids a wrong suggestion but may miss available compatible activities. Check useful matches and justified no-match cases, not only avoided errors.

Diagnose and reteach

CLAIM TO REPAIR Every classroom problem needs AI.

For guessing, return to given facts and request a trace. For vocabulary without application, use one example and one non-example. For arithmetic errors, check the setup before the calculation. Finish with a different uncoached case before recording mastery.

Address these additional misconceptions

Using AI is itself the outcome: State what improves for the user and how to observe it.

A mismatch count proves its cause: Separate observations from explanations that need further evidence.

No wrong suggestions means success: Check whether valid options were missed or every request was refused.

A prototype requires real personal data: This paper project uses permitted fictional requests and task-relevant inputs.

Explanations for the additional workbook examples

W1 Write the problem without prescribing a tool

Use the ten-request activity log to write a problem statement with a user, setting and observed difficulty. Explain why “Build an AI app” is not yet a problem statement.

For example: fictional pupils choosing activities in the supplied classroom exercise had to choose again in four of ten requests because the first choice did not fit the time or room conditions. Build an AI app names a solution or construction task without specifying a user difficulty or evidence of need.

W2 Identify claims beyond the evidence

A team says that the four mismatches prove every pupil dislikes the catalogue and that a recommender will fix the problem. Identify two unsupported conclusions and state what the log actually establishes.

The log establishes four incompatible first choices out of ten supplied fictional requests. It does not establish every pupil’s opinion, the cause of the mismatches or that an untested recommender will improve outcomes. Accept any two of those unsupported conclusions with the bounded observation.

W3 Choose information that serves the outcome

The paper project must suggest an activity compatible with available time and a quiet-room requirement. Choose relevant inputs from minutes available, quiet required, pupil name and home address. State the output and a simpler alternative to a recommender. Explain one boundary.

Minutes available and quiet required are relevant. Name and address are unnecessary. Output a compatible activity suggestion or an explained no-match result. A clearer catalogue or compatibility checklist is a simple alternative. Keep requests fictional and the action advisory; do not extend the task to collecting personal profiles or publishing choices.

W4 Frame a different problem independently

In a fictional equipment cupboard exercise, eight teams choose a box from a list. Three boxes do not contain the items required by the corresponding request; five do. The teacher permits a paper box-finder using fictional item requests and an inventory. Write a brief naming the user difficulty, evidence, desired outcome, necessary information, an excluded action and a simple alternative. Identify one cause or benefit that these counts do not establish.

Teams selecting equipment in the supplied exercise had three mismatches out of eight and need a box whose listed contents meet the request, or a clear no-match result. Use requested items and the inventory; personal identities are unnecessary. Exclude real purchases, changing stock or public posting. A clearer inventory or item checklist is a simple alternative. The counts do not explain why teams chose mismatching boxes or prove that a box-finder will improve selection.

W5 Measure help as well as avoided errors

For W4, a proposed box-finder never selects a wrong box because it always reports no match. Explain why counting only wrong suggestions would hide a failure. Name two results to record on test requests and explain why the original three-of-eight observation is not already an evaluation of the new tool.

Always refusing misses useful matches when suitable boxes exist. Record compatible or incompatible suggestions and whether no-match outputs were justified by the inventory, along with total requests. The three-of-eight observation describes the earlier selection process; the proposed tool has not yet been tested under a comparison procedure.

Feedback that the learner uses

Name the earliest unsupported step and give one action to repair it. Have the learner make that repair now and explain why. Then assess the relevant skill on a fresh, fully specified case. Use feedback, labs and repair paths

Use the two formative questions as supported practice, not as independent mastery scores. Discuss the deciding evidence rather than accepting a guessed selection.

The feedback comes from the reviewed public worked case. Do not reveal the separately reserved independent teacher answer until the learner has attempted that case.

Ask students to predict before running code. Preserve errors and actual outputs; collect a changed test and an explanation. The branch-boundary and indexing lessons deliberately include failure cases.

Use the earlier worked-explanation links to address a specific missing building block, then return to the current question. Adapt this suggestion after observing the learner; it is not an automatic diagnosis.

Collect the completed-work portfolio as evidence. Record support separately from correctness, and assess explanation, trace/test and limitations. Progress ticks and quiz selections alone do not establish mastery.
