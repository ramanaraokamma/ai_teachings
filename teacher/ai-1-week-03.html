Explain the mechanism

Explain the mechanism

Follow the information instead of imagining magic inside the machine.

INPUT light, sound, or heat from the environment

PROCESS a sensor converts the signal into recorded values

OUTPUT numbers or pixel/sound data sent to a later process

CORE IDEA Sensors measure signals; later processing gives those signals meaning.

UNDER THE HOOD A sensor is a transducer: it converts a physical quantity into a signal a computing system can represent. Sensing and interpretation are separate. A camera measures light; an image model may later infer a label.

Draw a fresh example with all three stages:

Every step connects a claim to observable evidence.

CASE A classroom weather station measures light, sound, and temperature but does not decide what those measurements mean.

STEP 1 — OBSERVE light, sound, or heat from the environment

STEP 2 — TRACE a sensor converts the signal into recorded values

STEP 3 — CONCLUDE numbers or pixel/sound data sent to a later process

MODEL REASONING 820 could be a light measurement, 24 C comes from a temperature sensor, and changing sound values come from a microphone. The numbers alone do not prove whether the plant needs water or whether a voice was present; later processing must interpret them.

DO NOT OVERCLAIM Do not say a sensor sees, hears, or understands. It measures and sends a representation.

What new evidence could change this answer?

Concrete teaching case

A light sensor reads 15 in a cupboard and 90 near a window. A microphone records a clap.

Which measures a light-related signal? Can either directly measure happiness?

Exact explanation to model

The light sensor measures light; the microphone measures sound. Neither directly measures feelings. Units and calibration matter when comparing devices.

Explain the added worked example

A garden display shows 24. Is that warm? We cannot interpret the number until we know what was measured and the unit. It might mean 24 degrees Celsius, 24 percent soil moisture according to a particular device, or 24 hours since watering. The screen alone does not supply the missing meaning.

A useful measurement record names the quantity, value, unit, time and relevant conditions. For air temperature, the location and exposure matter: a device in direct sunshine may warm differently from one in shade. Record the conditions rather than assuming that every nearby device measured the same situation.

Measurement is a way of representing part of the physical world. It does not capture everything about that world. One air-temperature reading does not tell us how dry the roots are, whether a plant is diseased or whether watering is permitted.

The sensor responds to a physical quantity. Electronics convert the response into a recorded value. A later program can compare the value with a threshold. Nothing about this path requires us to claim that the sensor understands plant health.

The rule uses exceeds 28, so a reading of exactly 28 does not trigger it. At 29 it does. This boundary is a property of the written rule; it is separate from the question of whether the measuring device is accurate enough for the intended purpose.

Model the reasoning aloud

Use the complete worked example below before asking learners to complete W2. Reveal a small part, invite a prediction, then explain the deciding step. These teacher notes contain solutions; do not reveal the independent case answer during modelling.

Two devices are placed side by side in a shaded room and allowed to settle. At three agreed times, device A reads 20, 21 and 20 degrees C; device B reads 22, 23 and 22 degrees C. B is 2 degrees higher on every comparison. That pattern is evidence of a consistent difference under these test conditions.

We cannot conclude from agreement patterns alone that A is correct and B is wrong. A suitable checked reference can help investigate. We also need to consider whether the devices have different response times, resolutions or placement. Three comparisons in one room do not prove performance in every temperature range.

Calibration is a reference check, sometimes followed by an adjustment. Do not simply subtract 2 from every future B reading because these three pairs happened to differ by 2. A correction needs support over the conditions where it will be used.

A pictorial worked case: Sensors Are Messengers

Two fictional light sensors disagree in a classroom. Compare them fairly rather than declaring one broken.

The facts we will use

On the same shelf, simultaneous readings are A: 40,50,60 and B: 45,55,65 in declared toy light units.

Neither device has been compared with a trusted reference.

Why this result follows

A repeated difference of 5 supports investigating an offset between devices. Matching time and location removes two obvious comparison problems. It does not show which device is correct: both could have errors. Preserve paired readings instead of adjusting them silently.

What this example does not establish

Three pairs do not prove an offset holds at every light level, nor identify the accurate device.

Demonstrate and guide practice

Demonstrate and guide practice

Board sequence

Write the given facts: A light sensor reads 15 in a cupboard and 90 near a window. A microphone records a clap.

Write the question to explain: Which measures a light-related signal? Can either directly measure happiness?

Reveal after predictions: The light sensor measures light; the microphone measures sound. Neither directly measures feelings. Units and calibration matter when comparing devices.

Keep for an independent attempt: A station reports light level 18 and temperature 22 degrees C. Which sensor produced each reading? Can either reading alone tell whether a child feels comfortable? Explain.

Classroom dialogue

Ask: Which facts are given, and which would we have to assume? Learners mark relevant details before discussing answers.

Say: Predict first. Show the rule, information path or calculation that would produce the result. A correct guess is not yet an explanation.

Reveal reasoning one step at a time. Explain why a tempting alternative fails. Write units and denominators for arithmetic, trace conditions for decisions, and identify supporting sources for claims.

In pairs, one learner solves while the other checks evidence; exchange roles. Remove the worked answer before independent assessment.

Model these guided questions

Put the devices together, use comparable settings and times, allow settling and repeat, or swap positions while recording conditions. If the difference follows position, location is plausible; if it follows device, investigate the device or settings. For the exit: ask what quantity and unit 30 refers to, when and where it was measured, and whether that measurement is suitable for the rule.

Diagnose and reteach

CLAIM TO REPAIR A sensor knows what a person feels.

For guessing, return to given facts and request a trace. For vocabulary without application, use one example and one non-example. For arithmetic errors, check the setup before the calculation. Finish with a different uncoached case before recording mastery.

Address these additional misconceptions

The more stable device must be accurate: A device can repeatedly report an offset value. Stability and closeness to a reference answer different questions.

The warning proves the condition in the world: Trace the recorded number into the rule. An incorrect or irrelevant input can produce a correctly computed warning.

Missing details can be filled with sensible guesses: Keep unknown fields explicit until evidence supplies them. Guesses must be labelled as assumptions.

Assess and explain the answers

Worked case answer

The light sensor measures light; the microphone measures sound. Neither directly measures feelings. Units and calibration matter when comparing devices.

Independent question

A station reports light level 18 and temperature 22 degrees C. Which sensor produced each reading? Can either reading alone tell whether a child feels comfortable? Explain.

A light sensor produced 18; a temperature sensor produced 22 degrees C. Neither directly measures a child's comfort. Ask the person rather than treating a measurement as their feelings.

Explanations for the additional workbook examples

W1 Repair an incomplete record

A notebook entry says 24 at lunchtime. Rewrite it as a useful air-temperature record by stating which missing fields must be collected. Do not invent values that were not observed.

Keep the observed value 24 and approximate time lunchtime; mark the unit, exact time if needed, location, exposure and device identity as unknown or to be checked. A suitable completed record needs air temperature, value, unit and relevant conditions. Invented degrees C or shade is not an observation.

W2 Trace a boundary

A warning rule is recorded temperature greater than 28 degrees C. State its output for 27, 28 and 29 degrees C. Explain why an off warning does not prove that the sensor is working correctly.

27 off, 28 off, 29 on. These outputs follow the comparison applied to the recorded values. A device could report an incorrect low value while the rule correctly stays off; measurement quality needs a separate check.

W3 Compare without choosing a winner

At the same three times, A records 18, 19 and 18 degrees C and B records 21, 22 and 21 degrees C in the same location. Calculate the paired differences. What is established, and what further evidence would help judge accuracy?

B minus A is 3, 3 and 3 degrees C. This establishes a consistent observed difference in these comparisons. It does not show which device is closer to the actual temperature. Use an appropriate checked reference, allow settling, record conditions and investigate across the intended range.

W4 Investigate a new measurement dispute

A classroom sound display near a speaker reads higher than one near the door. A student declares the door device broken. Propose a safe comparison using normal classroom sound. Predict one result consistent with location explaining the difference and one result that would leave a device difference worth investigating.

Place both devices side by side under the same normal sound conditions, using the same settings and reading times, or exchange positions and repeat. If readings become similar together or the higher reading follows the speaker location, location is a plausible explanation. If one device remains consistently lower side by side or after swapping, a device or setting difference remains plausible. Neither result alone proves a device broken; a checked reference and repeated observations help.

W5 Limit a system's conclusion

An air-temperature sensor reports 29 degrees C and a rule turns on a warning. Someone writes The plant is thirsty. Explain the unsupported step and name information more directly relevant to deciding whether watering is needed.

Crossing an air-temperature threshold does not establish root-zone dryness or a plant's watering need. Soil conditions, the kind of plant, recent watering and a gardener's inspection are more relevant. The warning can be correctly on while the thirst claim remains unsupported.
