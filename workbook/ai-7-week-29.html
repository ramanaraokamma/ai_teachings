01. Figures that preserve evidence: the case

Draw bars that preserve the comparison. A report plots correct counts for two versions on the same ten cases. Supplied facts: A has 6/10 correct; B has 8/10.

The count axis should start at zero and identify the ten-case denominator.

Point to the given fact that matters most. Explain why it matters.

02. Follow the deciding steps

Draw honest bars: Use heights 6 and 8 on a zero-based correct-count axis. → Label the scale: State counts out of ten, not an unspecified quality score. → Keep context nearby: Note the fixed tasks and absence of a significance test.

Point to each numbered stage and explain which recorded input it uses.

03. Zero-based correct counts

Zero-based correct counts. Units: correct cases out of 10. A: 6. B: 8.

Read the labels and values. Explain the relationship the model shows.

04. Change one thing. Trace what follows.

Original case: Note the fixed tasks and absence of a significance test. Changed condition: Start the axis at 5 without making the truncation obvious.

Expected result: The bars exaggerate the visual contrast and require correction or clear disclosure. Reason: Visible heights no longer correspond to the full count scale.

Predict before checking the worked changed-case result. Name what changed and what the supplied facts allow you to conclude.

05. Connect the explanation to its limit

Figures make evidence easier to inspect when labels and scales preserve meaning. A dramatic-looking visual cannot supply missing uncertainty or representativeness.

Limit: A well-labelled chart still cannot establish a causal or population claim from ten cases.

Explain the case, then point to a limit on the claim.
