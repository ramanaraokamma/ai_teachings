01. Operational Labels: the case

Two fictional catalogue reviewers label books compact or extended. Supplied facts: Agreed policy: compact means at most 80 pages; extended means more than 80. Reviewer A uses pages<80; reviewer B uses pages<=80.

Inspect books of 79,80,81 pages.

Point to the given fact that matters most. Explain why it matters.

02. Follow the deciding steps

Shared definition: At most 80 includes a book with exactly 80 pages. → Reviewer A: 79 compact; 80 and 81 extended. → Reviewer B: 79 and 80 compact; 81 extended. → Repair boundary: A must use <=80; retain the disagreement and changed policy implementation.

Point to each numbered stage and explain which recorded input it uses.

03. Operational label boundary

Operational label boundary. Book 1: Pages = 79, A labels = compact, B labels = compact. Book 2: Pages = 80, A labels = extended, B labels = compact. Book 3: Pages = 81, A labels = extended, B labels = extended.

Read the labels and values. Explain the relationship the model shows.

04. Change one thing. Trace what follows.

Original case: A must use <=80; retain the disagreement and changed policy implementation. Changed condition: Change the documented policy to fewer than 80 instead.

Expected result: A’s original labels now match; B must change its equality behavior. Reason: The authoritative definition changed, not the physical books.

Predict before checking the worked changed-case result. Name what changed and what the supplied facts allow you to conclude.

05. Connect the explanation to its limit

The reviewers agree on two of three examples, but that fraction does not decide which definition to keep. The documented policy decides the equality case. Repairing A’s implementation aligns its labels with the agreed meaning.

Rechecking 79 and 81 ensures the boundary fix preserves adjacent decisions. Limit: A shared definition improves consistency but does not prove the category captures every learner’s needs.

Explain the case, then point to a limit on the claim.
